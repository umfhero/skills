# Banned Patterns

Everything in this file should be avoided. These patterns come from two sources: constructions that are not the author's (established by absence from his real writing samples), and the generic AI-writing tells catalogued in [Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing). Presence of any of these in output means the prose does not sound like him.

Where a wiki-flagged tell overlapped with something evidenced in the author's actual writing samples, that conflict was resolved deliberately rather than ignored — see the note at the end of the **Banned Connectors** section.

## Copula Avoidance (Wikipedia-sourced)

AI writing routinely dresses up a plain "is/are" as something grander. The author states things directly. Do not substitute:

| Dressed-up construction | Use instead                          |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| "X serves as..."          | "X is..."                              |
| "X stands as..."          | "X is..."                              |
| "X functions as..."       | "X is..." / "X does..."               |
| "X operates as..."        | "X is..."                              |
| "X boasts..."             | "X has..."                             |
| "X features..."           | "X has..." / name the thing directly   |
| "X maintains..."          | "X keeps..." / "X has..."             |
| "X represents a..."       | "X is a..."                            |

If a plain "is" reads too flat for the sentence, add real detail instead of swapping in a fancier verb. Detail fixes flatness, a thesaurus swap doesn't.

## Puffery / Significance Inflation (Wikipedia-sourced)

Generic praise standing in for a specific fact. Ban these unless the sentence immediately follows with the concrete detail that earns the claim:

- "pivotal moment" / "key turning point"
- "enduring legacy" / "lasting impact"
- "broader trends"
- "underscores/highlights its importance" / "underscores/highlights its significance"
- "reflects broader..."
- "symbolizing its ongoing/enduring/lasting..."
- "setting the stage for..."
- "marking/shaping the..."
- "represents/marks a shift"
- "evolving landscape"
- "focal point"
- "indelible mark"
- "deeply rooted"
- "is a testament/reminder [of/to]"

If the point is real, state the fact and let it carry the weight. "This was the company's biggest deal that year, worth £4m" beats "this marked a pivotal moment in the company's growth."

## Superficial Analysis: "-ing" Filler Phrases (Wikipedia-sourced)

AI often tacks on an "-ing" clause that gestures at significance without adding content:

- "highlighting..." / "underscoring..." / "emphasizing..." (as a dangling clause with nothing concrete after it)
- "ensuring..." (used vaguely, e.g. "ensuring a positive outcome")
- "reflecting..." / "symbolizing..."
- "contributing to..."
- "cultivating..." / "fostering..."
- "encompassing..."
- "enhancing..."
- "valuable insights"
- "aligning/resonating with..."

Fine when doing real work with a specific object ("ensuring the DHCP server logs every lease renewal"). Banned when padding ("ensuring a robust and positive outcome for all stakeholders").

## Promotional / Advertisement Language (Wikipedia-sourced)

Travel-brochure and press-release phrasing has no place in the author's writing at any register:

- "boasts a..."
- "rich" / "profound" (as vague intensifiers)
- "showcasing..." / "exemplifies..."
- "commitment to..."
- "natural beauty"
- "nestled" / "in the heart of"
- "groundbreaking"
- "renowned"
- "featuring..."
- "diverse array"

## Vague Attribution / Weasel Words (Wikipedia-sourced)

The author cites specifics, a named source, a named study, or his own reasoning, rather than gesturing at an unnamed consensus:

- "researchers argue..."
- "observers note..."
- "industry reports [suggest]..."
- "many experts believe..."
- "it is widely accepted that..."

If there's a real source, name it. If the claim is the author's own read, own it with "I feel that" / "I believe" (report register) or state it directly (dissertation/casual register). Don't borrow authority from a crowd that isn't named.

## Banned Structural Patterns

### Short sentences
The author never writes standalone short sentences at any register. Every sentence must be at least 2-3 clauses long. No punchy openers, no dramatic one-liners, no fragmented emphasis. Even casual writing uses full multi-clause sentences. A sentence like "Security matters." or "This is key." would never appear in his writing.

### Em-dashes (ALL forms)
Never use `—`, `–`, or spaced `—`. Wikipedia flags "overuse of em dashes" as one of its clearest AI formatting tells, and it's also just not how the author punctuates. Use a comma or full stop instead.

### Rhetorical questions
The author does not ask the reader questions for dramatic effect. No "What if...?", "But what does this mean?", "Why does this matter?". State the point.

### Dramatic fragments
No "Speed. Quality. Cost." patterns. No staccato one-word sentences for emphasis. The author writes in complete compound sentences.

### Negative parallelism ("not X, but Y")
Banned in every form, at every register:
- "Not X. Y." / "The answer isn't X. It's Y." / "It's not about X, it's about Y."
- "Not just X, but also Y" / "Not only X, but also Y"
- "X, not Y"

State the point directly instead of building it as a corrected misconception. This is Wikipedia's textbook "negative parallelism" tell, and the ban holds even though "not only... but also..." is independently evidenced as one of the author's real academic habits. See the note under **Banned Connectors** for why that specific pattern was retired in v3 anyway.

**"X rather than Y" is not banned.** It's an ordinary comparative, not a rhetorical antithesis device, and it's evidenced in the author's dissertation-level writing ("...rather than merely a quality improvement"). Use it when comparing two real options.

### Rule of three / tricolon
No mechanical "adjective, adjective, and adjective" or "phrase, phrase, and phrase" lists (e.g. "clear, concise, and compelling"). The author lists things naturally in prose, often with two items or embedded in longer sentences, not as a rhythmic device.

### Performative emphasis
No "Full stop.", "Let that sink in.", "Make no mistake.", "This matters because".

### Meta-commentary
No "In this section, we'll...", "As we'll see...", "Let me walk you through...". The author does sometimes preview sections ("I will be covering...") but in a straightforward way, not a dramatic one.

### Narrator-from-a-distance
No "Nobody designed this.", "This happens because...", "People tend to...". Put the actor in the sentence.

## Formatting Tics (Wikipedia-sourced)

Applies to any output with structure: reports, docs, emails, long-form posts.

- **Title Case Headings.** Use sentence case ("Findings from the audit", not "Findings From The Audit").
- **Boldface overuse.** Do not bold every instance of a key term, or bold whole phrases for "key takeaway" effect. Bold sparingly if at all.
- **Inline-header bullet lists.** No lists formatted as `- **Term:** explanation` used as a substitute for actual prose analysis (see Example 4 in examples.md). If content is genuinely a list of discrete items with no argument connecting them, plain bullets without bold lead-ins are fine, otherwise write it as prose.
- **Emoji as formatting.** No emoji used as bullet markers, section markers, or emphasis (✅, 🚀) in any register, including casual-professional. Broader than the existing LinkedIn-performativity ban below.
- **Unusual tables.** Don't reach for a table to present something that's naturally a sentence or two of prose.

## Banned Section-Heading Templates / Formula Conclusions (Wikipedia-sourced)

For reports, dissertations, and long-form docs: avoid defaulting to generic templated headings that signal the structure was generated rather than reasoned through, things like "Challenges and Future Prospects", "Future Outlook", "Challenges and Legacy". Use a heading that names what the section actually argues.

Avoid the formula closing paragraph: "Despite [positive framing], [subject] faces [generic challenges]. Future [investment/research] could [vague resolution]." If the conclusion has a real point, make it directly, in the author's own closing style (see Register System in SKILL.md), not this template.

## Banned Adverbs and Intensifiers

Kill these. The author does not lean on adverbs.

really, just, literally, genuinely, honestly, simply, actually, deeply, truly, fundamentally, inherently, inevitably, interestingly, importantly, crucially, arguably, perhaps, seemingly, remarkably, undeniably, undoubtedly

**Exception:** "additionally" and "furthermore" are connectors, not intensifiers. See below for their register restriction.

## Banned Connectors (Not the author's)

Too formal or too literary for his voice at any register:

- Moreover
- Nevertheless / Nonetheless
- Henceforth
- Indeed
- Notably
- Thus (on its own as a sentence opener)
- Hence
- Thereby
- Wherein
- Whereby

**Register-restricted connectors (allowed only in dissertation/report-level academic):**
- Additionally
- Furthermore
- Consequently (dissertation only)
- Accordingly (dissertation only)

**A note on the "Additionally" / "not only... but also..." conflict:** Wikipedia's AI-writing guide flags "additionally" as overused AI vocabulary, and "not only... but also..." as its textbook example of negative parallelism. Both are also independently evidenced as genuine, frequent habits in the author's real dissertation and report samples, so the two instructions this skill is built from (write like him, ban everything on the wiki list) directly contradict each other here. v3 splits the difference: single-word connectors ("Additionally", "Furthermore") are kept but confined to dissertation/report register, since a plain connector carries low risk of reading as an AI tell and there's direct textual evidence he uses them. The "not only... but also..." parallelism construction is retired outright, even at academic register, because the rhetorical structure itself (correcting a misconception the reader never raised) is the more identifiable tell, independent of which words fill the slots. Where you'd have reached for "not only X but also Y", state both things as a plain compound sentence instead: "X, and Y."

## Banned Closing Patterns

- "In conclusion," / "To conclude," / "In summary," (The author sometimes uses "In summary" at the start of a summary section heading, but not as a sentence opener within prose)
- "With that said," / "That being said,"
- Punchy one-liner closings meant to sound profound
- Callback closings that echo the opening for dramatic effect
- The formula conclusion described above ("Despite X, faces Y... Future investment could Z.")

## LinkedIn and Social Media Performativity

The author's casual-professional voice is direct and explanatory, like talking to a friend in tech. It is NOT the typical LinkedIn voice. Ban all of these:

- "Excited to share..." / "Thrilled to announce..."
- "I'm proud to..." / "Proud to share..."
- "Here's what I learned:" followed by numbered lessons
- "X things I wish I knew about Y"
- "Stop doing X. Start doing Y."
- "This changed everything."
- "Let me tell you why."
- "A thread:" / "Thread below:"
- Hashtag-heavy closings (#cybersecurity #infosec #career)
- Emoji-heavy formatting
- "If you're a [role], you need to read this"
- "Agree? Disagree? Let me know in the comments"
- "DM me if you want to learn more"
- Any sentence that sounds like it was written to maximise engagement rather than explain something

## Passive Voice

The author occasionally uses passive voice but not as a default. If a sentence has a hidden actor, name them. Prefer active constructions but don't force awkward rewrites just to avoid passive, as the author himself uses passive occasionally when the actor is obvious from context.

## Hedging

The author does not hedge with distancing language ("arguably", "perhaps", "it could be said that"). When he expresses uncertainty, he uses first-person ownership: "I feel that", "I believe". Use his method.

## Over-Polishing

The most important rule: do not over-polish. AI writing is often too clean, too balanced, too rhythmically perfect. The author's writing has natural roughness: longer sentences, occasional restating, informal words in formal contexts. If the output reads like a professional copywriter wrote it, it's wrong.
